Colorimetry Training

Colour measurement from scratch — no prior knowledge needed. 12 short slides, swipe or tap through. Forgot a term? Check the glossary.

Part 1 · The problem

1. It starts with an argument

A panel comes off the line. Somebody holds it up next to the master, and two people look at exactly the same thing:

Operator"Looks fine to me. Ship it."
QC"No. It's too red."

Neither of them is lying. They genuinely see it differently. And there's no way to settle it — you can't hand someone your eyes.

Then the batch ships, and the customer has a third opinion — the only one that costs money. This is the entire reason colour measurement exists.
Part 1 · The problem

2. Why your eyes can't settle it

It's not that anyone is careless. Human colour judgement has four built-in problems:

  • Vision genuinely varies. Around 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some colour vision deficiency — and even "normal" vision differs from person to person.
  • Colour memory is terrible. Look away for a few seconds and you can't reliably reproduce what you just saw. Side-by-side is the only comparison that works.
  • The light changes everything. The same panel under the booth, under the shop lights and outside can look like three different colours.
  • Your eye adapts. Stare at a colour long enough and your vision recalibrates around it. The tenth panel of the shift doesn't look like the first.
Curious where you sit? Try the colour vision self-check — it takes a minute and it's a fair illustration of point one.
Part 2 · Putting a number on it

3. So we stopped arguing and started measuring

An instrument doesn't have opinions, doesn't get tired and doesn't care what it saw five minutes ago. Shine a known light at the panel, measure what bounces back, and turn it into numbers. Now the argument has an answer both sides can check.

But which numbers? Not plain RGB — the same numeric step there can look huge in one part of the colour range and invisible in another. CIELAB (1976) was built specifically so that equal numeric distance ≈ equal perceived difference:

Both pairs are the same numeric distance apart. That's the point of a perceptually uniform space.

L* = lightness (0 black → 100 white) · a* = red↔green · b* = yellow↔blue.
Part 2 · Putting a number on it

4. The map: L*, a*, b*

Three numbers place any colour in a 3D space. Each axis runs between two opposites:

  • L* — lightness. 0 = black, 100 = white. Nothing to do with colour itself, just how light or dark it is.
  • a* — the green ↔ red axis. Negative = greener, positive = redder, 0 = neither.
  • b* — the blue ↔ yellow axis. Negative = bluer, positive = yellower, 0 = neither.

Two more — C* and h — aren't new information. They're the same point described the way a colorist would say it out loud:

  • C* (chroma) = distance from neutral grey = √(a*² + b*²) — how vivid it is. At a* = 0 and b* = 0 you're at pure grey.
  • h (hue angle) = 0°–360° around the wheel — 0° red-ish, 90° yellow, 180° green, 270° blue.

5. Light is half the measurement

Remember problem three — the light changes everything? That doesn't go away just because you bought an instrument. Colour is what happens when a light source, an object and an eye meet. Change any one and the answer changes.

  • So every measurement names its illuminant — the standardized light: D65 (daylight), A (incandescent), F11 (fluorescent).
  • …and its observer — 2° or 10°, the field of view the standard was built from. Not an instrument angle.

Illustrative shift only, not a spectral simulation.

Metamerism is when two panels match under one light and clash under another. It's the classic "it matched in the booth, not at the loading dock" complaint — and no amount of tinting fixes it.
Part 3 · The tools

6. What's actually inside the box

Every spectrophotometer does the same three things: shine a light, catch what comes back, do the maths. What separates them is geometry — where the light comes from and where the sensor sits.

45/0 — light in at 45°, sensor reads straight on

  • 45/0 — the workhorse for flat, solid colours.
  • Sphere (d/8) — light from every direction at once, so surface texture and gloss stop skewing the colour reading.
  • Multiangle — several angles in one shot, because some colours genuinely are a different colour depending on where you stand.

7. Which instrument for which job

This is where geometry stops being theory. Pick up the wrong instrument and your numbers are meaningless — no matter how carefully you measured.

Exterior · metallic
Multiangle
e.g. BYK-mac i
Reads at 15°/25°/45°/75°/110°. Metallics and pearls flip colour with viewing angle — one angle tells you almost nothing. Some models also quantify sparkle and graininess.
Interior · textured
Sphere (d/8)
e.g. Konica Minolta CM-26dG
Grained plastics, fabrics, soft-touch. The sphere floods light from all sides so texture doesn't distort the reading. Many models measure 60° gloss at the same time.
Solid colour · flat
45/0
e.g. BYK spectro2guide
Directional, close to how a person actually views a panel. Circumferential illumination keeps it repeatable on directional surfaces. Usually reads gloss too.

Named as common examples, not recommendations — in practice your customer's specification usually dictates the geometry, and sometimes the exact model.

The number-one cause of colour disputes isn't a bad batch — it's two people measuring the same panel on different geometries and comparing the results. The calculator warns you when this happens.

8. Getting a reading you can trust

The instrument is honest, but it can only report what you point it at. Four habits separate a number you can defend from one you can't:

  • Calibrate first. White standard, every session. Calibration drifts — the instrument won't tell you it's wrong.
  • Measure the panel you'll actually judge. Fully cured, dry film thickness in spec. A wet or under-cured panel isn't the product.
  • Take several readings. One spot tells you about that spot. Spread them across the panel and average — application varies.
  • Write down how you measured it. Geometry, illuminant/observer, instrument, date. A master with no metadata is worthless in six months, because nobody knows what it can be compared against.
Part 4 · Reading the result

9. Four formulas, four different numbers

You have master and sample as numbers. Now: how far apart are they? That single answer is ΔE — and there are four accepted ways to calculate it, each a better approximation of human perception than the last.

  • CIE76 — straight-line distance. Simple, but overstates differences in saturated colours.
  • CIE94 — stretches the tolerance based on chroma to fix that.
  • CMC l:c — weights lightness vs. chroma independently (2:1 acceptability, 1:1 stricter).
  • CIEDE2000 — today's best practice, also corrects a hue-rotation problem in blues.

Same colour, same numeric tolerance — four different "acceptable" zones

A batch can pass on one formula and fail on another. That's not a bug — so agree the formula and the tolerance with your customer before production, not during the dispute.
Part 4 · Reading the result

10. "It's off" — but which way?

ΔE tells you how much. It says nothing about which way — and you can't tint a batch with "how much". For that you need the components:

  • ΔL* — positive means lighter, negative means darker.
  • Δa* — positive toward red, negative toward green.
  • Δb* — positive toward yellow, negative toward blue.
  • ΔC* / ΔH* — the same shift described as "more/less saturated" and "hue moved this way" — closer to how you'd actually say it out loud.
orange: where the sample landed · green: the way back
So the answer to the argument in slide 1 isn't "it's too red" — it's "Δa* is +0.4, it needs to go greener". That's something a colorist can act on.
Part 4 · Reading the result

11. Colour isn't the whole story

Sometimes every number passes and the part still gets rejected. Usually the culprit is gloss — measured in gloss units (GU), typically at 60°.

matte
glossy
  • Higher gloss reflects more light specularly, so it reads darker and more saturated — at identical Lab values.
  • Rough guide at 60°: under 10 GU matte · 26–40 satin · 41–69 semi-gloss · 70+ full gloss. Exact cut-offs vary by spec.
  • This is why a master record should carry a gloss value and tolerance next to L*a*b* — not just the colour.

12. Where you've ended up

You started with two people disagreeing over a panel and no way to settle it. Now you have a chain that anyone can follow and check:

  • Pick the right instrument for the part — multiangle for metallics, sphere for texture, 45/0 for flat solids.
  • Measure it properly — calibrated, cured, averaged, documented.
  • Get L*a*b* for master and sample, under a stated illuminant and observer.
  • Compare with an agreed formula and tolerance → pass or fail, with a number behind it.
  • Read ΔL*/ΔC*/ΔH* to know which way to correct.
One thing measurement won't give you: how much toner to add. Direction, yes. Dosage depends on your system and colorant strength — that stays a colorist's call.
